Determination of the viscosity of glycerine by the terminal velocity method

Falling-sphere viscometry · Stokes’ law · glycerine held at 20 °C · accepted value η = 1.41 Pa·s

Experiment brief Tap to collapse
Practical 4 — Terminal velocity and the viscosity of glycerine Level: A-Level / IB Duration: 45 minutes

1Aim

Determine the viscosity of glycerine by measuring the terminal velocity of a small sphere falling through it, then compare your result to the accepted value of 1.41 Pa·s at 20 °C.

2Theory

As the sphere falls it reaches a steady speed where the forces balance: its weight equals the upthrust plus the viscous drag.

43πr3ρsg  =  43πr3ρfg  + 6πηrv (1)

Rearranged for the terminal velocity:

vterm = 2r2g(ρs − ρf)9η (2)

Equation (2) assumes the liquid is unbounded and the flow is laminar (Reynolds number < 1). Both assumptions are checked for you on the bench.

3Apparatus

  • Glycerine — ρf = 1260 kg/m³, η ≈ 1.41 Pa·s at 20 °C
  • Steel, glass and PVC spheres of known material density
  • Vernier caliper (0.1 mm)
  • Digital balance reading to 0.0001 g
  • Measuring cylinder, internal radius 40 mm, clamped in a retort stand
  • Stopwatch, metre rule and thermometer

4Method

  1. Measure the radius. Pick up the vernier caliper, clamp the sphere in its jaws and read the diameter, then halve it for the radius.
  2. Find the density. Place the sphere on the balance and read its mass. Combine it with your volume to get ρs.
  3. Time the fall. Drop the sphere and press Space as its centre passes each of the four lines. This gives three independent times over three equal 100 mm intervals. Repeat for several trials and average.
  4. Calculate η. Use your mean terminal velocity in the rearranged formula and compare with the accepted value.

5Notes

  • The sphere is drawn enlarged so you can see it — read the real numbers from the instruments.
  • Terminal velocity is reached almost instantly in glycerine, so all three intervals should give the same speed. If they don’t, check your timing.
  • Advanced control: the walls of a real tube slow the sphere down. Switch the tube-wall correction on (in the apparatus panel) to see why a measured value runs high.

Tube-wall effect

With the correction on, the measured velocity is reduced by the tube walls: vmeasured = vterm / (1 + 2.4r/R) with R = 40 mm. Correct your velocity before using it: vcorrected = vmeasured(1 + 2.4r/R).
